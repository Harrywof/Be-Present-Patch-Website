Why it matters

Phones are everywhere. Attention needs backup.

The patch does not say phones are bad. It gives your brain a pause point before the check, scroll, camera grab, or notification reflex.

91%of U.S. adults own a smartphone, according to Pew Research Center. 46%of U.S. teens say they are online almost constantly. 237notifications hit teens' phones on a typical day in Common Sense Media's study.

Your phone can steal focus just by being nearby.

The Brain Drain study found that the mere presence of a smartphone can reduce available cognitive capacity, even when people are not using it.

Read the cognitive capacity study

Notifications interrupt attention even when ignored.

A study on cell-phone notifications found that alerts can hurt performance on attention-demanding tasks even when people do not pick up the phone.

Read the notification study

At work, interruptions cost more than a few seconds.

Research on workplace interruptions links interruptions with higher stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort. A physical barrier helps protect deep-work blocks.

Read the workplace interruption study

At shows, phones can change the room.

Phone-free comedy and event policies exist because recording, checking, and glowing screens can break attention for performers and the audience around you.

Read about phone-free comedy shows
